The short answer is: **mathematics does not yet know.**

This exact question is the basis of the **Navier-Stokes Existence and Smoothness
 Problem**, one of the seven famous **Millennium Prize Problems** established by
 the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, carrying a \$1,000,000 reward for a sol
ution.

Here is a breakdown of what we know, why it is so difficult to solve, and what i
t means for a fluid to "blow up."

---

### 1. The Core of the Problem
The motion of an incompressible fluid (like water at normal speeds) is governed
by the **Navier-Stokes equations**.

* **The Setup:** You begin with smooth, physically reasonable starting condition
s-meaning the fluid has finite energy, smooth velocity profiles, and no infinite
 speeds or tears.
* **The Question:** Will the mathematical solution stay smooth and well-behaved
for all future time ($t \to \infty$), or can the fluid self-concentrate energy i
nto an infinitesimal point, causing quantities like velocity, pressure, or vorti
city (spin) to become **infinite in a finite amount of time** (a "blow-up" or si
ngularity)?

---

### 2. Why is the 3D Case So Difficult?

The main mathematical culprit behind a potential blow-up in three dimensions is
a phenomenon called **vortex stretching**:

* **In Two Dimensions (2D):** Fluids cannot stretch vortices along a third axis.
 Vorticity is strictly conserved and transported. Because of this, mathematician
s (notably Olga Ladyzhenskaya and Jean Leray in the mid-20th century) proved tha
t **2D fluids will always remain smooth for all time.**
* **In Three Dimensions (3D):** Eddies and whirlpools can be pulled, stretched,
and folded in three dimensions. As a vortex tube is stretched, conservation of a
ngular momentum forces it to spin faster and narrower (like a figure skater pull
ing in their arms). Mathematically, this creates a nonlinear feedback loop where
 fluid velocity gradients can feed back into themselves.

The viscosity (internal friction) of the fluid works to smooth things out by dis
sipating kinetic energy into heat. The entire mystery boils down to: **Does visc
osity act fast enough to prevent vortex stretching from running away to infinity
?**

---

### 3. What We *Do* Know

While the complete question remains open, significant partial progress has been
made:

1. **Short-Time Smoothness:** If you start with smooth conditions, the fluid is
proven to remain smooth for at least a *short* period of time.
2. **Small Data:** If the fluid starts out moving very slowly (or the viscosity
is extraordinarily high), the solutions are proven to remain smooth for all time
.
3. **Weak Solutions (Leray, 1934):** Jean Leray proved that "weak" solutions exi
st for all time. These solutions satisfy the physics on average, but they may co
ntain singularities where the standard derivatives break down, and it is unknown
 if these solutions are unique.
4. **The Euler Equations (Zero Viscosity):** For an idealized fluid with *zero*
viscosity (the Euler equations), mathematicians have recently made breakthroughs
. In 2022, Thomas Hou and Jiajie Chen proved that the 3D Euler equations *can* b
low up in finite time from smooth initial data in a domain with a boundary. Whet
her this can happen in the viscous Navier-Stokes equations remains unsolved.

---

### 4. What Would "Blow-Up" Mean Physically?

If the equations do allow a blow-up, it does **not** mean real water would sudde
nly explode with infinite energy.

Instead, it would mean that the Navier-Stokes equations are an incomplete model
of nature. Navier-Stokes treats a fluid as a smooth continuum. If the equations
blow up, it means the fluid has concentrated down to such microscopic scales tha
t the continuum approximation fails, and quantum/molecular mechanics must take o
ver to describe what happens next.

### Current Consensus
Mathematicians and physicists remain split:
* **The "Global Regularity" camp** believes that viscosity is inherently strong
enough to always prevent infinite gradients.
* **The "Blow-Up" camp** (which has gained traction due to recent computer simul
ations and proofs on related equations) suspects that highly tailored, hostile i
nitial conditions could trigger a runaway singularity.
